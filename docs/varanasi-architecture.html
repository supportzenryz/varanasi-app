How Varanasi Is Built

The reasoning behind the restaurant's website and back office — what protects the money, what protects the guest, what happens when something goes wrong, and how each of those claims was checked rather than asserted.

9 September 2026 Prepared by Zenryz 916 automated checks passing

The position in one paragraph

Two restaurants, one system, no monthly ransom

Varanasi runs its own reservations, its own gift vouchers, its own menus and its own guest records, on software the restaurant owns outright. There is no booking platform taking a slice per cover, no separate voucher provider taking a slice per card, and no supplier holding the guest list. The trade-off is that the parts a platform would normally supply — taking money safely, keeping staff accounts honest, answering a data request, surviving a mistake — have to be built properly here instead. This document is the account of how they were, written so that a restaurateur can follow it and a technical reviewer can check it.

Part one

The seven rules this build follows

Every decision below came out of one of these. They are worth stating plainly, because they are what makes the difference between software that works on the day it is demonstrated and software that is still trustworthy in three years.

I

The database is the last word, never the form

Anything arriving from a browser is treated as a claim, not a fact — including the price, including which restaurant a voucher is valid at, including who the person says they are. A manager cannot mint £500 of the other branch's liability by editing a dropdown, because the branch is decided on the server from their account, not read out of the form.

II

Anything touching money is written conditionally

A redemption does not write "the new balance is £30". It writes "set the balance to £30 only if it is still £50", then reads the row back to confirm. Two tills redeeming the same voucher in the same second cannot both succeed — the second one is told the voucher changed and to try again.

III

Refuse rather than guess

When the system cannot be certain, it stops and says so. Payments configured wrongly in production refuse to fall back to the simulator. A session secret that has not been set refuses to let the server start at all. A silent wrong answer is worse than a loud refusal, because only one of them gets noticed.

IV

Every action leaves a name and a time

Price changed, voucher cancelled, account promoted, guest record erased — each writes a line naming the person, the thing and the moment. The owner is told straight away about anything that moves money or changes who can do what, and gets everything else in one daily summary.

V

Nobody has more power than their job needs

Staff redeem vouchers and see bookings. Managers edit them. Only the owner can cancel a voucher, change a price, add a colleague, read the activity log or erase a guest's record. Everyone below owner is pinned to their own restaurant and cannot see or change the other one.

VI

The recovery path is designed before the failure

Forgotten password, locked-out owner, corrupted database, a payment taken for a voucher the guest abandoned halfway — each has a route back that was written deliberately and tested. "That has never happened" is not a plan.

VII

A claim that isn't tested isn't a claim

Everything asserted in this document is exercised by an automated check that fails the build if it stops being true. The gift voucher's QR code is photographed and read back by a real barcode decoder, because a QR code that is subtly wrong looks perfect and scans as nothing.

Part two

The shape of the system

Five layers, each of which can only be reached through the one above it. The point of the arrangement is that a rule written once — "only an owner may cancel a voucher" — cannot be bypassed by finding a different screen.

The public site 22 page templates, rendered per branch: menus, drinks, private dining rooms, gallery, catering, contact, terms, privacy — plus booking, gift-voucher purchase and the printable voucher itself. Every page is branch-scoped, so Leicester's address, hours and rooms can never appear under Birmingham.
The back office 13 sections — menus, private dining, reservations, blocked dates, gift vouchers, enquiries, gallery, staff, settings, activity log, erasure requests, backups. Never indexed by search engines and never cached, enforced at the server, not by convention.
The rules 29 self-contained modules holding every decision the business makes: what a valid booking is, what a password may be, when a voucher expires, who may do what. The screens ask these; they do not re-implement them, which is why the till and the admin cannot disagree about a balance.
The record 17 tables — branches, menus, rooms, bookings, blocked dates, vouchers and their redemptions, enquiries, staff, settings, the activity log. Changes to its shape are applied as 7 numbered migrations, so the live database and a fresh one are provably identical.
The outside world Stripe for card payments, Resend for email, WhatsApp for staff alerts. Each is optional and each fails visibly: if email is misconfigured the server says so on startup, in English, rather than quietly dropping confirmations.

Part three

The money

Three things here take or hold real money: a deposit on a reservation, the purchase of a gift voucher, and the redemption of one at the till. Each is written on the assumption that the network will drop, the guest will press the button twice, and two members of staff will do the same thing at the same moment.

Buying a gift voucher

The guest fills in the form

Amount, who it is for, an optional message, an optional delivery date. The amount is checked before it is rounded to pence — asking whether a rounded number is a whole number tells you nothing.

A voucher is created as pending

It exists, it has a code, and it is worth nothing. No voucher ever carries a balance before the money has arrived.

Stripe takes the payment

On Stripe's own hosted page. No card number ever reaches this system, which is what keeps the restaurant out of the heaviest tier of card-industry compliance.

Stripe tells the server, and the server checks the signature

The message is verified as a genuine Stripe message before it is read at all — an HMAC over the exact raw text, compared in constant time, rejected if it is more than five minutes old. Anyone can send us a message claiming a voucher was paid for; only Stripe can sign one.

The voucher activates and is emailed

It gets its balance, its expiry, and a printable page carrying a QR code. If the same notification arrives twice — which Stripe deliberately does — the second one changes nothing.

Redeeming one at the restaurant

Staff either type the code printed on the voucher — VG- and twelve characters — or scan its QR — the QR simply opens the redemption screen with the code already filled in, so a phone camera and a keyboard reach the same place. The screen shows the balance, the restaurant it is valid at and the expiry before anything is taken.

The amount is then removed conditionally, against the balance the member of staff was actually looking at. A voucher that changed in between — because the other till was on it, or because the page had been open for an hour — is refused with an explanation rather than being overwritten. Every redemption writes its own line: amount, branch, member of staff, time.

The QR code was written from the specification rather than borrowed. That is normally a bad idea, and here it removes a third-party dependency from a piece of paper the restaurant hands to a paying guest. The risk is that an encoder with a single bit out of place produces something that looks exactly like a QR code and scans as nothing at all.

So it is not judged by eye. Every code the system can produce is rendered to an image and read back by an independent barcode decoder — the same class of software as a phone camera — and the text has to come back identical. That test found two real faults during the build: a transposed corner in the format bits, and a mis-scored mask pattern. Both would have shipped invisibly.

Part five

Passwords, and getting back in

How a password is kept

Passwords are not stored. What is stored is a bcrypt hash at cost 12 — a deliberately slow one-way transformation, tuned so that checking one password takes a fraction of a second and checking a stolen list of them would take a very long time. If the database file were taken tomorrow, the passwords in it could not be read out of it.

A new password must be at least twelve characters and is refused if it is the starting password, the previous one, an obvious choice, a run across the keyboard, built from too few distinct characters, or made out of the person's own name or email address. The same rules apply everywhere a password is set — the sign-in screen, the reset link and the command-line tool all ask one shared piece of code, so they cannot drift apart.

Staying signed in

A signed-in session is a signed token in a cookie the browser will not hand to JavaScript, will not send to another site, and — in production — will only send over HTTPS. It lasts seven days. It also carries a fingerprint of the account's password hash, which has a useful consequence: changing a password immediately invalidates every session that account has open anywhere. That is the correct behaviour when a laptop is lost or a member of staff leaves.

Three ways back in, in order

The person forgot their password

They ask for a link by email. The link lasts 30 minutes, works once, and asking for a new one cancels the earlier ones. Only a hash of the link's token is stored, so the same protection that covers passwords covers reset links. The screen says the same thing whether or not the address exists — otherwise it becomes a way to discover who works there.

An account is locked out by wrong guesses

Five failures against one email address locks it for fifteen minutes; fifty from one internet connection locks that connection. An owner can clear a lock immediately from the staff screen, so a real person who mistyped is never waiting on a timer.

Every owner is locked out at once

The recovery of last resort, and the one that is usually missing. A command run on the server — npm run staff:password — sets a named account's password directly. It obeys exactly the same strength rules as the website and it writes a line into the activity log, so using it is never invisible.

Two-step verification by phone was considered and deliberately deferred. It is worth adding once the restaurant is trading on this system, and it is a small piece of work on top of what is here — but it introduces a new way to be locked out, and adding it before the recovery paths above were solid would have made the system less safe rather than more.

Part six

The guest's data

Answering an erasure request

A guest is entitled to ask for their details to be removed, and the restaurant has a month to do it. There is a screen for this, and two decisions inside it are worth stating because they are the ones that are usually got wrong.

The record is anonymised, not deleted. A booking is a trading record — covers on a night, a deposit taken — and destroying it falsifies accounts that HMRC requires kept for six years. The law anticipates exactly this: the right to erasure does not override a legal obligation to retain. So the person comes out of the row and the transaction stays in it.

A gift voucher with a balance cannot be erased. It is money the restaurant owes to whoever holds the code. Removing the name does not cancel the debt; it makes the debt unattributable, which is worse for both sides. The screen says so, names the voucher, and does the rest.

Backups

A copy is taken every 24 hours and the last 30 are kept. Each copy is opened and read back after it is written, and the count of bookings inside it is reported, because a backup nobody has ever restored is a promise, not a protection. The owner can see the list, take one on demand, and download one.

The activity log

Every administrative action is recorded against a person and a time. Rather than emailing the owner four hundred times on a busy Saturday — which produces an inbox nobody reads — the log splits by consequence: anything that moves money, changes who can do what, or takes data out of the building is reported immediately; everything else arrives in one daily summary with nothing omitted.

What the browser is allowed to do

The site sends a content security policy that names the only places code and images may come from, refuses to be embedded in another site's frame, refuses to let a browser guess a file's type, and switches off access to camera, microphone, location and the browser payment API — none of which this site has any business using. The back office additionally tells search engines to stay out and forbids any page of it from being cached.

Part eight

Before it goes live

Six items. None is development work; all are configuration that can only be done once, with the real accounts, and each is deliberately something the system will complain about until it is done.

  • Set the session secret on the live server The production server refuses to start without it, by design.
  • Verify the restaurant's own sending domain Until then the server warns on every start that no confirmation email is being delivered.
  • Switch Stripe from test keys to live keys And confirm the Stripe account is in the restaurant's name, not a previous supplier's.
  • Point Stripe's notifications at the live address and roll the signing secret The signature check is worthless if the secret behind it is one anybody has seen.
  • Set the live site address It is what goes into voucher links, booking links and reset emails.
  • Change every starting password Staff are already forced to on first sign-in; this is confirming it happened.

Part nine

What is deliberately not built

Stating this is part of the architecture, not an apology for it. Each was considered and left out on purpose, and each is straightforward to add later because the groundwork is here.

Two-step verification for staff sign-in — deferred until trading, for the reason given in Part five. A native phone app — the back office already works properly on a phone, which is what the "app" question usually means; a genuine app store product is a different and much larger undertaking. A till or EPOS integration — redemptions are recorded here and reconciled by hand, which is correct until there is a volume that justifies wiring the two together. Loyalty and marketing automation — consent is captured and stored correctly, so the data will be there when the restaurant wants to use it.
