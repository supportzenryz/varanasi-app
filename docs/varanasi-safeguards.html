What protects your guests and your money

A plain-English account of how the reservations, gift vouchers and back office on varanasi.uk keep guests’ details private and the restaurant’s money where it belongs — and the handful of things only you can do.

Prepared by Zenryz · September 2026

Does the website ever see a guest’s card?

No — and this is the single most important sentence in this document. When a guest pays a deposit or buys a gift voucher, they are handed over to Stripe, who host the payment page on their own systems. The card number, the expiry date and the security code are typed into Stripe’s page, not ours. They never reach this website, they are never written to its database, and they are never in a backup.

That matters because it changes what a break-in could cost you. If somebody got into this website tomorrow, there would be no card numbers to find — because there have never been any. Stripe is certified to the payment industry’s highest level (PCI DSS Level 1) and is used by millions of businesses for exactly this reason.

What the site keeps is a reference from Stripe saying “this booking was paid for”. That reference is useless to anyone else: it cannot be used to charge a card again, anywhere.

How do you know a deposit was really paid?

Because Stripe says so, in a message the site can prove came from Stripe. Every payment confirmation carries a cryptographic signature, and one that does not match is refused. A forged “this was paid” message cannot confirm a table.

The amount is also worked out here, on the restaurant’s side, from the deposit rules you set in Settings — never taken from the guest’s browser, where it could be edited. A guest cannot change what they owe by fiddling with the page.

Refunds

A refund can only be issued by an owner or a manager, is checked against Stripe’s own record of what has already been refunded, and is recorded in the activity log with who did it. Pressing the button twice does not send the money twice; neither does a browser resending the request.

Can a gift voucher be spent twice?

No. Every voucher has its own balance and its own ledger: each redemption records the amount, the balance afterwards, which restaurant took it and which member of staff. A £100 voucher against a £64 bill leaves £36 on it, and the guest can come back for the rest.

Two things stop the same voucher being drawn down twice. The till has to send back the balance it was showing, and if the voucher has changed in the meantime the redemption is refused rather than applied — so a double-tap behind the bar, a browser “resend”, or two members of staff redeeming at the same moment can only ever take the amount once. And the balance is written with a condition attached, so even in the fraction of a second between reading and writing, only the first of two attempts lands.

The codes themselves

A voucher code is twelve characters from a thirty-two letter alphabet, chosen by the same kind of random generator used for encryption keys. There are more possible codes than there are grains of sand on Earth, so guessing one is not a thing that happens. The easily-confused characters — I, O, 0, 1 — are left out, so a code can be read out over the telephone without ambiguity.

Each voucher now also has its own page, which the guest can print or show on a phone. It carries a scannable code that opens the till screen with the voucher already looked up, so a member of staff can scan it or type it — whichever suits the moment.

Who can see what?

Every person who works on the site has their own account, and each account has a role. Managers and staff see their own restaurant only: a Leicester manager cannot open Birmingham’s bookings, menus, rooms or vouchers, and cannot spend Birmingham’s voucher balances. This is enforced on the server, not merely hidden on screen — the difference matters, because hiding a button is not the same as refusing the action.

How hard is it to get into the back office?

A password is never stored. What is stored is a one-way scramble of it (bcrypt), which cannot be turned back into the password even by us — so a stolen copy of the database does not hand anybody a way in.

  • Twelve characters minimum, with upper and lower case and a number. The starting password every new account is given is refused as a new one, so nobody can stay on it. Three or four unrelated words are the easiest thing to remember and the hardest to guess.
  • Five wrong passwords locks that account for fifteen minutes, and you are emailed when it happens. There is a second, wider limit per internet address, so somebody working through a list of staff addresses is stopped as well.
  • Signing in lasts a week, then asks again. Changing a password signs that account out everywhere immediately — which is the point of changing it, if somebody else had it.
  • Suspending an account takes effect on the next click, not whenever their session happens to run out.

Forgotten passwords

A member of staff can email themselves a link that lets them set a new one. The link works once, expires after thirty minutes, and asking for a new one cancels the previous link — so an old email left in a shared inbox is worthless. Only a scrambled copy of the link is stored, so a stolen backup contains nothing that opens an account. Every reset is logged and you are emailed the moment one is used, because a password reset nobody asked for is how an account takeover starts.

An owner can also reset any account directly, and there is a command-line tool as the last resort — for the case where nobody knows the owner password and email is not yet working. That was a real gap: resetting a password requires being signed in, so “ask an owner” is no help when you are the owner.

What happens to a guest’s details?

The site keeps what a restaurant needs to seat someone: name, telephone number, email address, party size, the occasion if they mention one, and any allergies or dietary needs. Nothing is sold, shared or sent anywhere else. Marketing consent is a separate tick that is never pre-ticked and never carried over from a previous form.

Allergy notes are treated as the most sensitive thing on the site, because under UK data protection law health information is a special category. They are shown to the kitchen and the people seating the table, and they are not in any page a guest could reach by guessing a booking reference — a booking’s details are shown only to somebody holding the private link we emailed them.

If somebody asks to be erased

An owner can answer a request under Article 17 from the admin. It rewrites that person’s records across the whole business, cannot be undone, and records that it happened without keeping the name or address it erased — only a fingerprint, enough to prove the request was answered and useless for anything else.

If something goes wrong, will you know who did it?

Yes. Every action that changes money, permissions or personal data is written to an activity log with the person, the time and what changed. You can read it in the admin under Activity.

Some things do not wait for you to look. A refund, a cancelled voucher, a voucher issued by hand, a change to payment settings, a staff account added or removed, a data export, an erasure, an account locked out by repeated wrong passwords, a password reset used — each of those emails you as it happens. Everything quieter arrives in one daily summary, so the interesting things are not buried in the ordinary ones.

What if the site or the data is lost?

A full copy of everything the restaurant has recorded — bookings, deposits, gift vouchers and their balances, enquiries, menus — is taken automatically every day and the last thirty are kept. Each one is opened and checked after it is made, because a backup nobody has ever opened is a hope rather than a backup. You can take one on demand and download it, and doing so is logged.

The site itself is hosted on Railway, served only over an encrypted connection, and the database lives on storage that survives a redeployment.

What stops a bored teenager with a script?

Every public form — enquiries, bookings, voucher purchases — is limited by how many times it can be used in an hour, counted three different ways so that changing one detail does not get round it. Without that, a loop in a browser could fill the enquiry list with thousands of fakes, burn through the restaurant’s email allowance, and hold a Saturday night’s tables hostage with bookings nobody pays for.

The site also tells browsers not to allow it to be framed inside another site, not to guess at file types, and to load scripts only from itself — so a script that somehow got onto a page would have nowhere to send anything it read.

What this does not do

Being straight about the edges is part of the point of a document like this.

  • It cannot stop someone you have given access from misusing it. What it does is make it visible: every action carries a name and a time, and the ones that matter email you as they happen. Give people the smallest role that lets them do their job, and never share an account.
  • There is no second factor on sign-in today. An authenticator app for owners and managers is the obvious next step and can be added; it is a decision about how much friction is right on a shared iPad at the pass, not a technical obstacle.
  • Email delivery depends on the email provider. The site now checks with the provider which sending domains it will accept and says so on screen, because the one failure that has actually cost this project days was every confirmation being silently refused while the screen said all was well.
  • No system is unbreakable. The aim is that a bad day is recoverable and visible: nothing worth stealing in the database, a full backup every night, and a log that says what happened.

The five things only you can do

Everything above is built and running. These are the ones that need a person with the passwords, and the first three matter before the site takes a real booking.

  1. Change the owner password. New installations start on a shared password that is written down in the project notes. Sign in, change it, and let the site email each member of staff their own link rather than telling them a password over the phone.
  2. Finish the sending domain. Confirmations, gift vouchers and staff alerts all go out by email. The provider will only send from a domain you have verified with them, and the sending address in Settings has to be on that domain — the admin now tells you which domains it will accept.
  3. Switch Stripe from test to live. Both keys together, and a payment webhook for the live domain. Until then no real money moves. The site refuses to pretend: with no payment provider configured it tells guests to ring the restaurant rather than confirming a table nobody has paid for.
  4. Keep the two secrets secret. The Stripe webhook signing secret and the session signing key live in the hosting settings and nowhere else — not in an email, not in a screenshot, not in a chat window. If one is ever pasted somewhere it should not be, roll it; it takes a minute.
  5. Give people the smallest role that works. Front-of-house needs staff. A head of service needs manager. Almost nobody needs owner. Suspend accounts the day somebody leaves — it takes effect immediately.

One sentence to remember

There are no card numbers in this system, there never have been, and the worst day it could have is one you can see in the log and undo from last night’s backup.
